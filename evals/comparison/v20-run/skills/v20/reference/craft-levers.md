# Craft levers

Removing AI tells gets you to "not obviously a robot." It does not get you to "good." This file is the difference: ways to restructure what the author already gave you so the piece pulls the reader forward.

**The guardrail that governs every lever below:** these are techniques for arranging and sharpening the draft's own material. They never license supplying content. Numbers, names, scenes, quotes, outcomes, and personal experience come from the draft or the user. A writing sample lends its voice, never its facts. When a lever wants a detail the draft lacks, write the plainer version and flag the gap ("a real example here would help"), or ask. An invented specific is worse than a vague true one, because a reader who catches it stops trusting everything else.

The throughline: **write like you are explaining something to a smart, skeptical friend.** That friend will not sit through throat-clearing, will not believe a vague claim, and will tune out the second you sound like a brochure.

## 1. The headline does most of the work

For anything with a title, headline, subject line, or hook, draft several options from the draft's own claims and pick the strongest. The best one usually beats the others by a wide margin, and you cannot find it by writing one and stopping.

A strong formula: **action verb + the specific outcome the draft states + a contrast or timeframe the draft states.** If the draft gives no outcome, use the plainest true description instead of a promise.

- Weak: "Improving your launch process"
- Strong, when the draft says launches got faster: "Launch in days, not weeks"
- Without a stated outcome: "How the launch checklist works"

Concrete and true beats clever and vague.

## 2. The opening line has one job: earn the second line

Not to summarize. Not to set context. Just to make the reader keep going. Four openings that work, each built from material the draft already holds:

- **A direct challenge:** "You are backing up the wrong files." (only if the draft makes that claim)
- **A small scene:** only if the draft contains one.
- **The specific result:** the draft's own best number or outcome, said first.
- **A confession:** only if the author actually confessed it in the draft or to you.

When the draft has none of these, open with its most concrete claim stated plainly. Never open with "In today's fast-paced world" or "Throughout history" or any sweeping windup.

## 3. Use the numbers the draft already has

Vague problems feel unsolvable; counted ones feel fixable. If the draft supplies figures, put them where the reader meets them early, and do the arithmetic for the reader when it is straightforward.

- Draft: "We spent four hours on emails, six on the landing page, and twelve on copy and screenshots."
- Better: "That is 22 hours, most of a work-week."

If the draft supplies no figures, do not make some up to "quantify the pain." Say what is true in plain words, and flag that a real figure from the author would strengthen it.

## 4. The "so what" chain

Most writing dies by stating a feature and stopping. Push each claim down until it reaches what a person feels, usually time, money, or worry, and write from the lowest rung the draft's own content supports.

> Fast database
> → queries load instantly (what the draft says)
> → users don't bounce (only if the draft makes this claim)

Stop where the draft stops. The point of the chain is to find the sentence worth keeping inside the author's own material, not to extend it with benefits the author never claimed.

## 5. Open loops

Tease something the piece genuinely resolves later, so the reader has to keep going. "I will get to why that backfired." Use these only when the draft really does come back to the point. Two to four per page; more reads as manipulative, which is its own tell.

## 6. Rhythm is the giveaway, in both directions

Real writing alternates. A short, blunt sentence. Then a longer one that takes its time, adds a clause, circles the idea, and lands somewhere slightly unexpected. Then maybe a fragment.

Same-length sentences and same-length paragraphs are the loudest signal that a machine wrote it. When you finish a draft, look at the shape of the paragraphs on the page. If they are all the same rectangle, break them up.

## 7. Social proof and quotes: never manufacture them

If the draft contains a testimonial, a quote, or a customer story, you may tighten its wording while keeping what it claims, who said it, and every figure in it. If the draft contains none, do not write one. A "real-sounding" invented testimonial is a fabricated quote attributed to nobody, and it is the fastest way to turn a rewrite into misinformation.

If a flat "Great product!" is all the draft has, say in the flags that a specific line from a real customer (what they had before, what changed, how long it took) would do much more work, and leave the sentence as it is.

## 8. The avoid list and the read-aloud test

Cut on sight: "delve," "utilize," "leverage" as a verb, "game-changer," "in today's world," "let's dive in," and a scatter of em dashes faking rhythm (see `ai-tells.md` for the full set).

Then the final test, the one that catches everything else: read it out loud. If it sounds like a textbook, a press release, or a chatbot being helpful, it is not done. Rewrite until it sounds like a person who knows the subject talking to someone they respect, and who is saying only what the draft says.
