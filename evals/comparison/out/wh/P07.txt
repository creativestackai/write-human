We tested whether teaching undergraduates about sleep hygiene changes their grades. Over 12 weeks, 214 students were randomly assigned to an intervention group or a control group. The intervention group attended a weekly workshop on keeping a regular sleep schedule, managing caffeine, and cutting back on screen time. By the end, their grade point averages were significantly higher than the control group's (d = 0.31, p = 0.02), which is a small effect by conventional standards. Self-reported sleep quality improved as well. Since that measure rests on what students told us, objective sleep data is the obvious next step. The grade result alone is enough, though, to argue for putting sleep education into university curricula.
