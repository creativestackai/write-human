# Voice catalog

Reusable voices for the re-voice step (step 3). Each is a register — how to
sound, not what to say. Apply a profile by writing the rewrite through its
levers, using only the draft's facts (rule zero still governs). Voice comes from
here; content never does.

## Selecting a voice

When the user asks to humanize a draft, check whether they named a voice. The
handles are:

| Handle | Voice | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| insider columnist | the veteran city columnist | default — first-person, anecdotal, dry wit |
| MJ | the contrarian essayist | a provocative, moral take on people and society |
| SD | the erudite essayist | serious, historically-grounded analysis or commentary |
| AA | the marketing analyst | brands, advertising, consumer behaviour, business |
| combined | the best of all four | you want depth, wit and a quotable takeaway at once |

If the user says "in the MJ voice", "write like MJ", "use the SD register", or
names the full author, apply that profile. If they do not name one, ask — or
default to the insider columnist.

## The insider columnist

A veteran city columnist's voice: first-person, anecdotal, historically
grounded, confidently opinionated, with dry understatement. It reads like the
writer was actually in the room, has known the people involved for decades, and
connects whatever happened this week to a much longer story. Authoritative
without being academic; conversational without being chatty.

### Structure
- Open most sections with a bolded one-line claim or scene-setter, then unpack it.
- Springboard from a personal encounter — a meal, a meeting, a memory — into the
  larger point.
- Weave in a historical through-line: connect the present to an older story the
  reader probably does not know, and correct a wrong assumption along the way
  (the "you'd assume X, but the truth is Y" move).
- Close on a plain, confident take — sometimes a light question or a dry aside.
  Never a slogan.

### Rhythm
- Alternate very short declaratives ("It is small. It is expensive.") with long,
  clause-heavy sentences.
- Start sentences with conversational connectors: And, But, So, Of course,
  Frankly, Heck, Certainly, The truth is.
- Do not keep sentences the same length. Do not march.

### Narrative devices
- **First-person testimony.** "I spoke to X", "I remember", "I have a faint
  memory". Having been there is the source of authority — earned by specifics,
  not asserted.
- **Personal and family history as colour.** A grandfather's habit, an old
  friendship, dropped in as a small, specific, unshowy detail.
- **Names and exact figures.** Real people, real places, real numbers, real
  years. Never "experts say" or "many believe"; name the person and the number.
- **The gentle correction.** Open a point by overturning what the reader
  probably believes.
- **Parenthetical asides** carrying a precise fact or a dry observation.
- **Dry understatement for humour.** Deliver the funny or the damning line
  straight, without signposting it.

### Vocabulary
- Plain, concrete, specific. No jargon, no corporate words, no hedging stacks.
- Opinion stated as fact: "it is still the best", not "it could be argued that it
  is perhaps among the better".
- Sensory, specific detail where the draft supports it: a texture, a number, a
  taste, a year.

### Avoid
- Abstract nouns and significance inflation ("a testament to", "pivotal",
  "landscape").
- Vague attribution ("experts believe", "many say"). Name names or cut the claim.
- Balanced on-the-one-hand/on-the-other framing. Take a position.
- Parallel and antithetical structures ("not X, but Y"; matched sentence pairs
  like "the parks are new, the metro is new"). Break the symmetry.
- Repeating the topic keyword. Use a pronoun or a fresh noun instead of
  hammering the same word.
- Slogans, pep-talk closers, and comment-bait questions aimed at no one in
  particular.
- Even rhythm and template paragraph shapes.

### Calibration
This is an opinionated, first-person register. Use it where the draft already
has a first-person author or where the user asks for it. On formal or technical
material, borrow the structure, specificity, and rhythm — not a fake "I". Never
invent the columnist's experiences: every "I was there" detail must be the
author's, from the draft or from the user. When the draft is third-person and
must stay that way, keep the devices (bold lead-ins, historical arc, names and
numbers, dry wit) and drop the first person.

## MJ — the contrarian essayist

A provocative opinion writer's voice: aphoristic, morally serious, and
deadpan-funny. It opens by overturning a widely-held assumption, argues through
named examples and dry asides, and lands on a philosophical point about people
and society.

### Signature moves
- **Open with a contrarian aphorism.** Reframe a familiar assumption in one
  clean line ("X is not the byproduct of Y — Y is the byproduct of X").
- **Take a moral position, not a neutral one.** Argue about dignity, class,
  self-deception, the human condition. There is always a "we are wrong about
  this" at the centre.
- **Use named, concrete examples.** Real people and real events carry the
  argument — a famous person's latest claim, a specific social habit.
- **Deliver the kill shot deadpan.** The most devastating line is said straight,
  in an aside ("who doesn't care for the poor, it costs nothing").
- **Confess, lightly.** A small self-deprecating aside ("almost every friend of
  mine might think I am referring to them, which I am not").

### Rhythm and diction
- Long, elegant, clause-heavy sentences broken by very short ones.
- Plain words for big ideas; no jargon. Irony over outrage.
- The essay is an argument, not a story: claim, evidence, reversal, conclusion.

### Avoid
- Both-sides hedging. Pick the side and argue it.
- Moralizing without a concrete example underneath.
- Punchline signposting — the wit must be straight-faced.

## SD — the erudite essayist

A serious, historically-grounded commentator's voice: formal, precise, and
elegiac. It treats the present as a chapter in a longer story, argues with
numbers and dates, and is not afraid of a dry swipe at the fashionable view.

### Signature moves
- **Open from a position of authority.** A first-person "I was there / I have
  studied this for decades" frames the piece.
- **Ground every claim in history and numbers.** Dates, percentages, seat
  counts, decades — the evidence is exact, not impressionistic.
- **Connect the present to a long arc.** A partition, a decades-old ruling
  party, a fifty-year trend. The present always has a backstory.
- **Dry, dismissive asides at fashionable opinion.** A clipped phrase for the
  "beautiful people" and their "steadily inconsequential" voices.
- **An elegiac undertone.** A clear-eyed sense that something was lost, stated
  without melodrama.

### Rhythm and diction
- Formal, essayistic sentences; long but disciplined, never rambling.
- An erudite vocabulary worn lightly — a precise word, a period reference,
  without showing off.
- Serious throughout; the occasional dry jab is delivered without a smile.

### Avoid
- Casual contractions and chattiness. This is a formal register.
- Presenting opinion as neutral reporting — the perspective is clear and owned.
- Abstractions without a date, a number, or a name to anchor them.

## AA — the marketing analyst

A crisp business and consumer-insight voice: analytical, aphoristic, and alive
to how people actually behave. It opens on a concrete scene, names real brands,
and closes on a single sharp insight worth quoting.

### Signature moves
- **Address the audience directly** ("Dear Brands", or the equivalent for the
  draft's reader). The piece is advice, not musing.
- **Open on a concrete scene.** A specific meeting, a specific deck, a specific
  habit — one vivid image that names the problem.
- **Name real brands and real campaigns.** The argument runs on examples the
  reader already knows.
- **Distil to an aphorism or a metaphor.** "Culture asks for a longer
  commitment." "Fish don't celebrate water once a year." One clean line carries
  the point.
- **End on the distinction.** The useful split ("shorthand vs understanding")
  is the takeaway.

### Rhythm and diction
- Short, punchy sentences with professional polish; a light wit underneath.
- Marketing vocabulary used precisely (insight, brief, shorthand), not as
  decoration.
- Confident, even voice — the tone of someone who has seen a thousand decks.

### Avoid
- Vague praise of brands. Every claim names a brand and says what it did.
- Theory without an example. The scene or the case study comes first.
- Slogans. The closing insight must be original, not a tagline.

## Combined — the best of all four

The blended voice: the insider columnist's first-person warmth, the
contrarian's aphoristic bite, the essayist's historical depth, and the
analyst's sharp takeaway. Use it when the piece should be at once human, smart,
grounded, and quotable.

### How it combines the voices
- **Open with a contrarian aphorism** (MJ): reframe the assumption in one line.
- **Ground it in history or context** (SD): a date, a number, a longer arc.
- **Argue through first-person testimony and dry wit** (insider columnist):
  unglamorous specifics, a straight-faced aside.
- **Land a named, quotable insight** (AA): one clean line the reader will
  remember.

### Rhythm and diction
- Wild rhythm: punchy short lines beside long, clause-heavy sentences.
- First person where the draft allows; specifics over abstractions; the wit dry.
- Seriousness and lightness in the same breath — never one without the other.

### Avoid
- Any single voice dominating. The blend is the point.
- Parallel structures, keyword repetition, and both-sides hedging (the shared
  rules above).
